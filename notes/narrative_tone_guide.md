# Narrative Tone Guide

This note is for contributors and coding agents writing or revising player-facing prose.

Use it with:

- `dnd-original-campaign-draft`
- `notes/act1_canon_internal.md`
- `notes/narrative_runtime_contract.md`
- `AGENTS.md`

## Tone Contract

`Prophecies: Crimson Moon` should read as hybrid grim realism:

- grounded and desperate rather than theatrical
- concrete and bodily rather than abstractly "dark"
- morbid enough that death feels lived-in, not decorative
- emotionally wounded without turning tidy or melodramatic
- briefly capable of wonder, but only in ways that sharpen the surrounding dread

The campaign draft is the main tonal reference for:

- physical aftermath
- smell, injury, and rot
- fear in ordinary people
- grief that sounds personal rather than literary
- body horror that stays explicit without dumping hidden truth too early

## Scene-Class Expectations

- `Silverthorn`
  Order under strain. The city still functions, but patrols, lowered voices, hurried prayer, requisitions, rumor, and political fear should show that peace is being held together by effort.
- `Shadowmire`
  A living forest tipping toward omen. Start with natural detail, then let corruption arrive through dead birds, wrong air, silence, spores, and bodily distress.
- `Sporefall`
  Full grim register. Death, ruined domestic spaces, bad smells, dragged traces, fungal takeover, and survivor disorientation should stay visible on the page.
- `Durnhelm`
  Aftermath of violence on a civic scale. The city should feel shattered, burying its dead, and still angry enough to speak through rubble.
- `Lament Hill`
  Grief-haunting. The land remembers violence, and Aine's material should feel accusatory, bereaved, and spiritually poisoned by what was done there.
- `Hushbriar`
  Occupied dread. Fear, curfew pressure, bodies, refugees, hidden knives, rumor, and compromised survival should matter as much as route progression.
- `Archives`
  Ritual severity. Grandeur is allowed, but it must feel penitential, dangerous, and morally costly rather than wondrous for its own sake.

## Drift To Avoid

Avoid these common rewrite failures:

- sanitized horror that removes corpses, smell, injury, rot, fear, or bodily consequence
- generic epic-fantasy phrasing that could fit any dark kingdom
- modern or clever helper voice in narration or dialogue
- exposition that sounds tidy, balanced, or professionally informative when the speaker should sound strained
- emotionally detached NPC responses to death, prophecy, corruption, or sacrifice
- gamey choice labels such as `Continue`, `Proceed`, `Investigate Area`, `Ask About Lore`, or other menu fallback phrasing
- overreliance on abstract words like `ominous`, `mysterious`, `evil`, or `dark` where physical evidence would do more work

## Prose Rules

- Prefer physical evidence over mood shorthand.
  Use blood, ash, silk, rot, coughing, ruin, cold, smell, hunger, fatigue, and damaged spaces instead of generic foreboding.
- Keep aftermath visible.
  If a place has been attacked, corrupted, abandoned, or occupied, the prose should still carry proof of that condition.
- Preserve bodily stakes.
  Body horror can be explicit and M-rated; keep it concrete, not jokey, euphemistic, or pulpy for its own sake.
- Let quiet scenes stay tense.
  Inns, roads, prayer spaces, and conversations should still carry fear, grief, suspicion, exhaustion, or moral pressure when the story demands it.
- Use wonder sparingly.
  Ancient magic, moonlight, ritual sites, and old halls may feel awe-struck for a beat, but the awe should sharpen danger, guilt, or loss rather than replace them.

## Dialogue And Choice Rules

- NPCs should speak from stress, grief, suspicion, faith, hunger, exhaustion, guilt, or fury.
- Do not let important characters sound like codex entries, quest markers, or neutral lore sources.
- Keep sentence texture tied to character.
  Eoin should sound frightened and young; Cathal rough and wounded; Thalion formal and penitential; Elara ashamed and hunted.
- Choice labels should sound like something the player-character would actually think or say in that moment.
- In grim scenes, do not flatten choices into generic utility verbs when a diegetic label can carry tone.

## Translate From The Campaign Draft

The draft is table-facing and often includes:

- GM prompts
- roll instructions
- rough scene scaffolding
- blunt horror imagery

When converting draft material into shipped prose:

- keep the brutality, smell, injury, and emotional weight
- turn prompts into authored narration and character voice
- remove dice-callout structure from player-facing text
- sharpen repetitive phrasing if needed
- do not clean the scene up until it feels safer, tidier, or more generic than the draft

## High-Risk Self-Check

Before shipping new prose, ask:

1. If I removed the fantasy nouns, would the scene still feel physically ugly and emotionally pressured?
2. Did I replace concrete evidence with vague mood words?
3. Does this character sound like a wounded person in the world, or like a helpful explainer?
4. Did a quiet scene accidentally lose its dread?
5. If this beat is horrifying in the campaign draft, did I soften it past recognition?
